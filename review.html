Can a 5% sample pick the best index configuration?

A reviewer's guide to the COL868 project. It assumes you are seeing the work for the first time, and it gives the problem, the design, every important decision with its reason, all headline results, and the places where I think the work is weakest.

PostgreSQL 14, TPC-DS scale factor 1, 99 queries, 33 index configurations, 5% samples, one seed. Results dated 5 to 6 October 2026.

One-paragraph summary

Choosing database indexes by the optimizer's cost estimate is unreliable, and building every candidate on a big database to measure it is too slow. We asked whether a small 5% sample of the database can rank candidate index sets the way the full database does. Six LLMs proposed index sets, and we timed 33 of them on the full database and on seven different 5% samples. The samples rank the configurations about as consistently as the full database ranks itself on a repeat run (Kendall tau around 0.45 to 0.5 against a noise ceiling of 0.45). Five of seven samples pick the same configuration, which is the second-best on the full database and only 1.4% slower than the best. They cost 25 to 90 times less for the random samples, and only about 3 times less for the join-aware ones. Join-aware sampling did not beat plain random sampling, and copying the full database's statistics onto the sample made rankings worse. The configurations differ by very little (31 of 33 are within 4% of each other), so the evidence is real but weak, and it comes from one seed, one rate and a database that fits in memory.

1. The problem

Indexes and index tuning

An index lets a database find rows without scanning a whole table, like the index at the back of a book. The right set of indexes can speed up queries enormously, and the wrong set wastes space and slows writes. Index tuning chooses that set for a given workload, which is a collection of queries.

How tuners judge a candidate, and why that is a problem

Tuners do not build candidate indexes. They ask the optimizer a "what-if" question: what would this query cost if these indexes existed? PostgreSQL's extension for this is HypoPG. The answer is an estimate, and estimates can be wrong. Wu et al. (SIGMOD 2025, papers/01-wu-qpr-sigmod2025.pdf) show that the configuration with the lowest estimated cost can run slower than the alternative once the indexes are actually built. They call this query performance regression (QPR). They report that about 11% of indexes created by Azure SQL's auto-indexing had to be reverted for this reason, and that most serious regressions are plan-shape changes, especially a new expensive nested-loop join.

The obvious fix and why it is too expensive

Build each candidate for real, run the workload, and keep the fastest. That is accurate, but on a 100 GB database the index builds and the query runs take days.

This project's idea

Do the real build-and-run on a small sample of the database, and check whether the sample picks the same winner as the full database.

The closest prior work is SQL:Trek (Airbnb, PVLDB 2025), which builds candidate indexes on a uniform 5% simulation database and runs the workload to prune false positives. It uses one sampling recipe and mostly transactional workloads. It does not measure agreement with the full database or compare sampling strategies. Details are in docs/related-work.md.

2. Research question and what is being claimed

Question (from the assignment, problem_statement.md). Do index-selection decisions made on sampled databases match those from full-database execution-time measurements, and which sampling strategy is most effective?

What this work claims

  • At SF=1 on PostgreSQL, 5% samples reproduce the full database's near-top choices and rank to within the measurement noise of the full database itself.
  • Among the strategies tried, join-aware and statistics-copying variants did not improve on plain random samples.

What it does not claim

  • It does not claim a sample reliably finds the exact best configuration. It does not, in 5 of 7 cases.
  • It does not claim anything about 100 GB databases, SQL Server, or sample rates other than 5%.
  • It does not claim any sampler is better or worse than another. The confidence intervals overlap.

4. The protocol

Authoritative text: docs/protocol.md. Diagrams: protocol.html.

1. CandidatesSix LLMs x 5 configurations, plus no index, plus HypoPG top-5 and top-8. 33 configurations after de-duplication.
2. Full database (D)For each configuration: create its indexes (timed), run all 99 queries, drop the indexes.
3. Ground truthRank the 33 by total workload time. Repeat the whole thing once more to measure noise.
4. Samples (D')Build 5% samples with several samplers.
5. Same measurement on D'Same 33 configurations, same 99 queries, same rules.
6. CompareKendall tau, top-1, regret, top-3, and cost reduction.

A configuration is a set of 1 to 8 indexes meant to serve the whole workload. A configuration is scored by the sum of its 99 query times. The index build time is recorded separately and used in a second score.

6. The LLM candidates

Prompt: advisors/prompt.md. Output: results/advisors/*.json.

  • Models: Claude Opus 5, Claude Fable 5.1, GPT 5.6 Sol, Gemini 3.8 Flash, Grok 4.7, and Composer 2.5 as a deliberately weaker baseline.
  • Constraints in the prompt: B-tree indexes only, up to 4 key columns plus an INCLUDE list, no partial or expression indexes, nothing on tables under 1000 rows, no duplicates of existing indexes, and make the 5 configurations meaningfully different.
  • After cleaning: 33 configurations in total, and every index builds. Unknown columns, tiny tables, existing indexes and duplicates are dropped by scripts/build_configs.py.
  • The LLMs never see the sample or any data. The sample is used afterwards to test their suggestions.

Independence check FACT

The advisors had file access to the whole repo even though the prompt named three inputs. I read each advisor's transcript for the files it opened. Five models opened only the allowed inputs. Gemini's first run also opened earlier measured results (the study set and HypoPG shortlist), which are exactly the kind of result this experiment tests. That output is kept in results/advisors_excluded/ and is not used. Gemini was rerun with an explicit allowed-file list, and the rerun's transcript is clean.

Another procedural slip is documented in docs/lab-notes.md: leftover indexes from an interrupted run made the configuration builder silently drop 26 indexes (198 became 172). It was caught because the total changed and the configurations were rebuilt. A guard in build_configs.py is still on the to-do list.

Why independent sampling is risky. Sampling store_sales and item independently at 5% keeps only about 0.25% of their join matches, which is why join-aware samplers were included. The Bernoulli sample row counts are what they should be (for example store_sales 143,451 of 2,880,404, which is 5.0%). Build times in the table are the sampling step only. The full build including keys and VACUUM took roughly 100 to 120 s.

What the statistics transplant leaves inconsistent. The data and the indexes on the sample are small, but the statistics say the table is full size. The planner therefore gets full-size selectivities with small-index costs. This is a hypothesis for the result in section 9, not something that was tested separately.

MetricMeaning
Kendall tau-bRank agreement between the sample's ordering of the 33 configurations and the full database's. 1 is the same order, 0 is unrelated, negative is reversed. The "b" version handles ties. The 95% interval is a bootstrap over the 33 configurations.

9.4 What the samples actually agree on DERIVED

I computed the true-best's and each pick's position from the result files for this guide.

  • On all five native samples, the sample's top two are the same two configurations as the full database's top two, with ranks 1 and 2 swapped in four of them (Bernoulli, page, universe, FK closure). Stratified has them in the right order. The third place differs (full-database rank 14 in all five samples).
  • With full statistics copied on, Bernoulli pushes the true best down to sample rank 11, and Universe's pick becomes a configuration that is only 11th on the full database.
  • Workload time on the no-index configuration: 190.7 s full, 4.4 s Bernoulli, 2.1 s page, 72.7 s universe, 53.6 s FK closure, 55.2 s stratified. The last three keep the dimension tables whole, which is where their lower savings come from.

10. Why the numbers look like this DERIVED

This section is my own reading of the per-query rows in truth_sf1.jsonl and goes beyond what the project docs say. Please check it before relying on it.

The two winners win mainly by fixing one capped query

Comparing each configuration's per-query times with the no-index run:

  • The two best configurations (Grok small dimension filters, Opus light dimension covering) each bring Q6 from the 20 s cap to about 10 s and Q14 from 7.0 s to about 3.7 s. Their timeouts drop from 7 to 6.
  • The third-best (Grok cross-channel) fixes only Q14. That alone is worth about 3.4 s, and it lands at 183.7 s.
  • Both winners put indexes on the filtered columns of date_dim (d_year, d_moy, d_month_seq, d_date). Q6 filters on exactly those columns. I have not run an ablation to prove the causal link, so treat it as likely.

What follows.

  • Most of the 33 configurations are effectively equal. The order among the middle 20 or so is mostly noise, and that is why tau cannot exceed about 0.45 even on a repeat.
  • The real signal is coarse: "does this configuration fix Q6 and Q14?" The samples agree on that, which is why they find the same top two.
  • The winner's true advantage is understated, since Q6 is censored at 20 s. Its real unconstrained time is unknown.
  • The 2.4 s gap between first and second on the full database (1.4%) is the "regret" of four of the five samples. Whether the sample picks first or second is nearly a coin flip, and the repeat run has the same two at 177.3 and 184.9 s.

Why cost reduction differs so much between samplers

Bernoulli and page shrink every table, including dimension tables, so everything runs 40 to 90 times faster. Universe, FK closure and stratified keep dimension tables in full, so queries that are dominated by dimension scans still cost a lot, giving only about 3x.

12. Threats to validity RISK

  1. One seed and one sampling rate. Every sample number is one random draw. Differences between samplers of 0.05 in tau are well inside the intervals.
  2. Tiny differences between configurations. 31 of 33 within 4%, and the ceiling is 0.45. The experiment has limited power to separate samplers even in principle.
  3. Censoring. Seven queries are capped under every configuration, and Q6 (a main differentiator) is capped under the empty configuration. Real gains are understated.
  4. Scale. SF=1 fits in memory, so I/O behaviour at scale is not tested. Queries on the sample that take milliseconds may behave differently from the full ones. The earlier SF=10 work was a per-query pilot, not a protocol result.
  5. Different engine from the assignment. PostgreSQL's planner, not SQL Server's. Sampling and statistics behaviour may differ.
  6. Measurement drift. The second full run was uniformly 5 s slower. The first-run ranking is itself one noisy draw, and the comparison target is that single run.
  7. Candidate pool. 33 configurations from six LLMs, heavily clustered. Wide diversity (clearly bad configurations, clearly good ones) would make ranking easier and may change conclusions. The empty configuration is the only clearly bad one.
  8. Statistics transplant is crude. Statistics are copied by column name and indexes are not rebuilt to match. A negative result is for this implementation, not for the idea in general.
  9. The Q6/Q14 explanation in section 10 is inferred from per-query times and not tested by ablation.
  10. Not compared against optimizer cost. The motivation relies on Wu et al. We did not re-measure how often HypoPG's pick differs from the runtime pick on this workload.
  11. Not independent of the author. The protocol, the scoring and the interpretation were chosen by the same person who ran the experiment. Several choices (score, cap, repeats) were informed by the earlier pilot data, so they are not blind to it. The history is in docs/lab-notes.md.

13. How the work got here (so you can judge the process)

  • Early pilot (September). Per-query ranking of single HypoPG indexes on an index-sensitive subset of queries, on 5% samples. Agreement with the full-database winner was 27 to 40%.
  • Repeat showed the target was noisy. The full database re-run against itself agreed on the winner only 47% of the time per query, with tau 0.32. The pilot therefore could not separate sampler quality from noise, and the conclusion was not to claim success or failure.
  • Q72 retraction. An early positive result on one query (Q72) was withdrawn after cache state, unfrozen statistics and timeouts were found to explain it. Those problems became the measurement rules above. Retracted files are marked in docs/files.md.
  • SF=10 attempt. A cold first pass produced speedups of 1000x that were just cache warmth. A warm re-screen kept 21 of 25 queries. Single queries ran for hours, which is why the protocol moved to SF=1.
  • 5 October redesign. I noticed the work had drifted from the protocol (per query, single indexes, no LLM, filtered queries). The experiment was redone as specified: configurations, whole workload, LLM advisors, all 99 queries.
  • Plan skeleton check. In the pilot, 143 of 151 query-index pairs had a different plan shape on the Bernoulli sample than on the full database. A matching winner is therefore not evidence of a matching plan.

14. Guidance for you as the reviewer

What I most want feedback on

  1. Is the headline defensible? "5% samples rank about as well as the full database ranks itself, and pick a configuration 1.4% from the best". Given the small spread and one seed, would you phrase it differently?
  2. Is the ranking score the right one? A sum of 99 times is dominated by Q6, Q14 and the capped queries. Would you prefer a per-query comparison, a log-ratio score, or only the uncapped queries?
  3. Should the cap be raised or removed? It hides the size of the main effect. The cost is wall-clock time.
  4. Is the noise ceiling argument fair? I use one repeat run as the ceiling. Would you want more repeats before treating 0.45 as the bound?
  5. What would convince you the statistics-transplant result is real or an artifact?

Questions to ask that I think are the most revealing

  • If the full database's own repeat gives tau 0.45, what would a sample have to score to count as "worse" than the full database?
  • Does the sample keep its top two because the sample is faithful, or because Q6 and Q14 are so large that anything notices them?
  • If 20 of the 33 configurations are indistinguishable, is the ranking metric measuring the sampler at all, or mostly measuring noise among ties?
  • Would a candidate pool with more clearly good and clearly bad configurations change the answer?
  • Do the conclusions survive a different seed, rate (1%, 10%), or scale (SF=10)?

What would change my mind

  • Several seeds per sampler showing a consistent ordering between samplers would turn the "no difference" finding into a real result in either direction.
  • A repeat full run with a narrower noise band (for example after fixing the machine-level drift).
  • An ablation of the two winners without the indexes that touch Q6 and Q14. If the ranking among the rest collapses, the results are about two queries, not about index quality in general.

Suggested next experiments (in order)

  1. Three to five seeds at 5%, with the same 33 configurations.
  2. 1% and 10% samples, as the assignment asks.
  3. Repeat the full database a third and fourth time, to tighten the noise ceiling.
  4. Raise the cap (or measure the capped queries separately) and re-score.
  5. SF=10 for the best two or three samplers, on a reduced configuration set.
  6. A workload-aware sampler that keeps the rows touched by the filter predicates.
  7. The same study on SQL Server, as the assignment specifies.

Review checklist

  • The protocol in docs/protocol.md is the same one that the code in scripts/eval_configs.py implements.
  • The same rules (cap, repeats, statistics) were applied to the full database and to every sample.
  • Advisor transcripts show no access to measured results (Gemini's first run is excluded for that reason).
  • Every claim in sections 9 and 11 has an interval or a "one seed" note next to it.
  • The numbers in the tables above match results/rank_sf1_p5.md.
  • The deviations from the assignment in section 2 are acceptable.
